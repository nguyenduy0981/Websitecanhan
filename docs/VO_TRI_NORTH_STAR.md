# VÔ TRI — The North Star: How VÔ TRI Should Feel in Five Years

> Companion to `docs/VO_TRI_GAMEPLAY_VISION.md`, but deliberately not an
> extension of it. That document describes mechanics, moments, language,
> mythology, and evolution. This one describes none of that. It exists
> to answer a single question whenever a future decision is uncertain:
> **does this make VÔ TRI feel more like this, or less?** If the honest
> answer is "less," the decision is wrong — no matter how good it looks
> on any metric, any roadmap, or any pitch.
>
> Nothing below is a feature. Nothing below is a specification. This
> document is meant to outlive every specific mechanic named anywhere
> else in this repository.

---

## The Atmosphere

Five years in, opening VÔ TRI should feel less like opening an app and
more like walking into a room where a conversation has already been
going on without you. Nothing announces itself. Nothing over-explains
itself. There's a mascot everyone has an opinion about and nobody fully
agrees on, jokes that assume you already know the first half, a court
that's been in session longer than you've been a member.

New content still arrives — daily, weekly, sometimes in bursts nobody
could have predicted — but the feeling underneath all of it is
continuity, not freshness. The product shouldn't feel *maintained*. It
should feel *lived-in*.

The dominant emotional register isn't excitement. It's the specific,
warm relief of being somewhere that already knows you a little — not
because it profiled you, but because it has embarrassed you, judged you,
and surprised you often enough that it has a relationship with you,
rather than a record of you.

## What Veterans Believe

Ask ten people who've been here since year one what the best Court
verdict of all time was, and you'll get ten different, confidently
argued answers — most of them wrong by any measurable standard, all of
them sincerely defended. That disagreement is the actual health check: a
culture that's been curated into consensus is dead. A culture still
arguing about its own history is alive.

Veterans believe the early days were funnier, or simpler, or made more
sense, or made *less* sense, depending who's talking — and neither side
has ever managed to convince the other. They believe they found the
Court before it was worth finding, an untestable claim nobody bothers
disputing, because disputing it would mean admitting they care.

Most tellingly: veterans rarely brag about level or points. They brag
about a specific verdict, a specific roast, a specific dare gone wrong
three years ago that a friend still won't let them forget. Their status
in the community was never measured by anything the app itself counts.

## How Newcomers Feel

Mildly behind, and glad about it. A newcomer's first week should feel
less like being welcomed and more like being tolerated with affection
while they catch up — overhearing a phrase they don't understand yet,
watching a verdict render and not getting why it's funny until someone
explains the joke underneath the joke. That gap is not a UX problem to
smooth away. It's the first real evidence they've found something that
existed before they did — something to earn their way into, not
something handed to them on signup.

The moment it flips — the first time a newcomer says "để Toà xử" to a
friend without thinking about it, or gets a Mirror moment specific
enough to actually sting a little — is the moment they've stopped
visiting and started belonging. Nothing in the product should announce
that transition. It should just happen, the way it happens in any real
community, noticed only in hindsight.

## The Stories People Tell

Nobody tells a story about their level. Nobody tells a story about a
leaderboard rank. The stories that survive — at a reunion, in a group
chat years later, to a partner who's never opened the app — sound like
this:

- "Yesterday my friend completely fooled me, and I didn't find out until
  the verdict came back."
- "I can't believe what it called me out on — how did it even know
  that."
- "We laughed for ten straight minutes over a five-second dare gone
  completely wrong."
- "I didn't screenshot it fast enough, and nobody believed it ever
  happened."
- "Remember the Tết where that whole thing happened with the Court? We
  still bring that up."

None of these stories are about the product. They're about *what
happened to a specific person, because of another specific person,
inside the product.* If a future feature can't eventually produce a
sentence shaped like these, it isn't advancing this vision — no matter
how impressive it looks on its own.

## What Must Never Change

Every mechanic named anywhere in `VO_TRI_GAMEPLAY_VISION.md` is
disposable. None of it is sacred. What has to survive intact, even if
every feature this vision describes is eventually replaced by something
better:

- **No experience ever requires only one person.** The day VÔ TRI
  becomes something a person does alone, it has quietly become a
  different, lesser product than the one this document describes.
- **Losing well is worth more than winning quietly.** A culture where
  being wrong, clueless, or embarrassed is a source of belonging rather
  than shame is the entire premise the tagline makes. The day that stops
  being true, the tagline becomes a lie the product tells about itself.
- **Nothing is ever fully explained.** The day a lore page resolves the
  mythology, or an FAQ demystifies the Prophecy's timing, the product has
  traded a living culture for a wiki. Mystery has to be actively
  protected against the product's own instinct to be helpful.
- **Nobody is ever mocked by the product itself.** Every roast, every
  verdict, every callout comes from affection, never contempt — a line
  that, once crossed even once at real scale, does not come back.
- **History is never deleted, decayed, or buried by an algorithm.**
  Whatever a user builds — a streak, a verdict, an inside joke, a place
  in the Chronicle — stays real and findable for as long as the product
  exists. A product that forgets its own users' pasts has no standing to
  ask them to build a future inside it.
- **Being clueless stays a skill, not a flaw, even once the product is
  sophisticated enough to stop saying so out loud.** The tagline can
  disappear from the marketing copy in five years. The feeling it
  describes cannot.

---

*If a future decision — a feature, a partnership, a monetization idea, an
engineering shortcut — would make any of the six things above less true,
the decision is wrong, regardless of what it would do to any metric.
This is the only paragraph in this document that should never be
revised without the owner's explicit, deliberate decision to change what
VÔ TRI fundamentally is.*
